What I learned

Describe your design: why these routers, LANs and links, and how you chose the addresses.

My network is a ring of four routers, r1 to r4, with one LAN each: h1 on r1, h2 on r2, h3 on r3 and h4 on r4. The ring is the point. Every router has two neighbours, so any single router-to-router link can fail and the other way round the ring is still physically there. That is the redundancy the brief asks for; whether the routes can use it is a separate question, and with static routes the answer is usually no.

The address plan is grouped in pairs to make summarisation possible. h1 and h2 sit inside 10.50.0.0/22 (10.50.1.0/24 and 10.50.2.0/24). h3 and h4 sit inside 10.50.32.0/20 (10.50.32.0/24 and 10.50.33.0/24). Router-to-router links are named after the pair they join: 10.50.12.0/30 for r1-r2, 10.50.23.0/30 for r2-r3, 10.50.34.0/30 for r3-r4, and 10.50.41.0/30 for r4-r1. On each link the first router named is .1 and the second is .2, so on the r4-r1 link r4 is .1 and r1 is .2 - the opposite of every other link. That is why r4's summary route points at 10.50.41.2 and not .1. Getting that backwards cost me the first build: it reported that r4's next hop was not on any network r4 is connected to, which is the check doing its job.

Which route summarises several LANs? Which networks does it cover, why is it correct, and what else would it attract if you added a network inside its range?

There are two, one for each pair of LANs. On r1 and r2, 10.50.32.0/20 covers h3's LAN (10.50.32.0/24) and h4's LAN (10.50.33.0/24). On r3 and r4, 10.50.0.0/22 covers h1's LAN (10.50.1.0/24) and h2's LAN (10.50.2.0/24). Each covers two of the four LANs, not all of them.

They are correct because each range contains only the LANs it is meant to cover and none of the other two. 10.50.32.0/20 spans 10.50.32.0 through 10.50.47.255, which does not touch .1 or .2. 10.50.0.0/22 spans 10.50.0.0 through 10.50.3.255, which does not touch .32 or .33. The router's lookup confirms it: on r1 both 10.50.32.10 and 10.50.33.10 go out via the same next hop, 10.50.12.2, so one route is answering for two networks.

The cost is that a summary is too broad. If I later added a LAN inside 10.50.32.0/20 - say 10.50.34.0/24 on a new router between r3 and r4 - r1 and r2 would already have a route for it via the /20, sending that traffic towards r2 before anything checked whether the LAN is really that way. It might still work if the rest of the network is right, but the summary has quietly made the route table less honest about where that network lives. That is why real networks group LANs by region before they summarise: summarise only what shares a topology, not just what shares a prefix.

When you took a link down, what happened, and why couldn't your routes recover even though another path existed? What would have recovered?

I took the r1-r4 link down and traced h1 to h4. The trace reached r1 and r2 and then stopped. The forward path was not actually broken: h1 still goes r1, r2, r3, r4, h4, because r1's route to h4 is the summary 10.50.32.0/20 via 10.50.12.2 - r2, not r4. What broke was the way back.

r4's only route to h1 is the summary 10.50.0.0/22 via 10.50.41.2, and 10.50.41.2 is r1's end of the link I had just taken down. r4 has no alternative, so replies never leave r4. The router still believes the route is usable: r1 ip route get 10.50.33.10 returns "via 10.50.12.2 dev r1-eth1" as if nothing happened. Static routes are installed once and never recomputed, so a link failure only breaks the routes that named that link, not the ones that now need to know about it.

A routing protocol would recover: OSPF on r4 would stop hearing from r1 on that link, recompute from what it still hears, and install 10.50.0.0/22 via r2 instead. Without one, r4 would need a second static route for the same network via 10.50.34.2 with a higher metric, which is the manual version of the same idea - a backup that takes over when the primary's link is gone.

In your network, what was the control plane? Who worked out the routes, and what does that mean for a network of 40 routers?

I was the control plane. Every route in network.yaml was worked out by me and typed as data: eight routes over four routers, each one chosen to send traffic the right way around the ring and to line up with the summaries. Nothing routed on its own. Until I added the first route, h1 could not reach h2 even though h1 and h2 are two hops apart.

That is the Week 1 problem. One wrong next hop becomes a black hole; one wrong summary becomes a loop that swallows traffic quietly. check_paths() catches both before anything is built, it follows every pair of hosts through lookup() the way the routers would, and reports the first router with no route or the first router it visits twice. On loop.yaml it names the loop r1, r2, r1; on missing.yaml it names r2 as the router with no route to 10.50.1.10.

But it cannot invent an address plan, or know that a summary was the wrong choice for this topology. At 40 routers it does not matter how good the check is - the authoring is the bottleneck. The right control plane for 40 routers is not more typed routes; it is either a routing protocol that discovers the topology and computes paths (OSPF or IS-IS), or a controller that holds the whole topology and installs routes per router. Both move the decision from my fingertips into software that can be reviewed, versioned and tested, which is exactly what Weeks 2 and 3 are building towards.
